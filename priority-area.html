Priority area

Extreme heat

California’s hottest days are getting hotter, more frequent, and longer-lasting, and the health burden falls hardest on outdoor workers, older residents, people without reliable cooling, and communities where tree cover and shade are scarce. Heat illness is also badly undercounted: emergency visits and deaths are often coded to the underlying condition heat aggravated rather than to heat itself.

We track heat-related emergency department visits and hospitalizations against local temperature records, so counties and community organizations can see who is being affected and where — not just that a heat wave happened. That surveillance feeds directly into heat-action planning with the communities carrying the most risk.

Heat is the clearest example of why our three sides of work belong together. The surveillance data shows where illness clusters; the community partnerships explain why (night-time temperatures, housing stock, shift work); and the projects turn both into cooling-center siting, outreach, and local heat-action plans.

Why it matters

3×

Hot-day ED visits

Heat-related emergency visits run roughly three times higher on extreme heat days than on typical summer days.

1 in 4

Inland residents

Live in a census tract with both high heat exposure and low tree canopy cover.

Nights stay hot

Rising overnight minimums remove the recovery window that makes daytime heat survivable.
